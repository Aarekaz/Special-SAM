\begin{abstract}
Camouflaged object segmentation is a demanding setting for testing whether foundation segmentation models can use weak texture and boundary cues. We ask a simple question: is SAM limited more by its decoder than by its encoder? On COD10K, freezing the ViT-H encoder and tuning only the 4M-parameter mask decoder raises mIoU from 57.2\% to 73.8\%. The gain holds across prompt types, reducing prompt sensitivity from a 3.5$\times$ range to 1.1$\times$, and mixed point/box training outperforms point-only and box-only alternatives by 2--4 and 19--37 mIoU, respectively. The effect also survives a trivial GT-free fixed center prompt, where mIoU improves from 0.326 to 0.566. For promptable camouflaged segmentation, these results point in the same direction: the frozen encoder already captures useful cues, while the default decoder does not reliably turn them into masks. We evaluate primarily under SAM's standard prompt-based protocol, using ground-truth-derived simulated clicks rather than a fully automatic COD setting.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
SAM~\cite{kirillov2023sam} handles many ordinary segmentation cases well, but camouflage exposes a sharper failure mode. On COD10K~\cite{fan2020cod10k}, base SAM reaches 57.2\% mIoU with center-of-mass prompts and only 20.5\% with edge prompts. In these scenes, the object is present, but separating it from the background depends on weak, spatially distributed evidence that is easy to miss. Camouflage is therefore a good way to ask what a foundation segmentation model already knows, and where adaptation actually matters.

That is the question we use it for here: \textbf{is the decoder the bottleneck?} SAM's frozen ViT-H encoder may already encode cues that matter for camouflage, while the general-purpose decoder may fail to turn those cues into object masks. If that is right, then adapting only the mask decoder should recover a substantial fraction of the missing capability without modifying the encoder. That is what we observe. Decoder-only fine-tuning yields a +16.6 mIoU gain on COD10K, and the gain transfers across several prompt types rather than depending on a single prompting rule.

This is also where our paper departs from prior SAM adaptation work, which often improves difficult domains by modifying the encoder, adding adapters, or redesigning prompt pathways~\cite{chen2023samadapter,hao2024comprompter}. We use camouflage less as an end task and more as a controlled testbed for locating the adaptation bottleneck. The issue is not simply how to maximize COD performance, but how far one can go by changing only the existing decoder.

The clearest evidence is not just the average gain, but the change in behavior across prompts. Base SAM is highly prompt-sensitive, varying from 0.205 to 0.727 mIoU across our four prompt strategies. After decoder specialization, this narrows to 0.694--0.776. An ablation shows that this robustness is learned rather than accidental: mixed point/box training is consistently stronger than point-only and dramatically better than box-only. We read that as a sign that the specialized decoder is learning object structure under subtle cues, not merely memorizing prompt geometry.

\noindent\textbf{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}
    \item We propose camouflaged segmentation as a stress test for subtle visual concept formation in SAM and ask whether the missing capability lies primarily in the decoder.
    \item We show that decoder-only adaptation of SAM yields a +16.6 mIoU gain on COD10K while keeping the encoder frozen.
    \item We show that prompt robustness is a key piece of evidence for this claim: mixed-prompt training reduces prompt sensitivity from 3.5$\times$ to 1.1$\times$ and outperforms point-only and box-only alternatives.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Method}
SAM consists of an image encoder, a prompt encoder, and a mask decoder. We freeze both encoders and train only the mask decoder (\cref{fig:architecture}). To keep training lightweight, we pre-compute image embeddings from the frozen ViT-H encoder and cache them as \texttt{.npy} files. Each training iteration uses either a point prompt (random foreground pixel) or a box prompt (tight ground-truth box) with 50/50 probability. That mixed regime helps avoid overfitting to a single prompt format and gives us a direct way to test whether any gain transfers across prompt styles.

We train with a 50/50 BCE+Dice loss, AdamW, cosine learning-rate decay, and 15 epochs. At test time, we evaluate four prompt strategies of increasing difficulty: Center-of-Mass, Edge, Multi-Grid, and Multi-Random. Our focus is not on fully automatic COD, but on what promptable evaluation reveals about SAM's internal bottlenecks.

\Cref{tab:main_viscon} gives the main result. Freezing the encoder and adapting only the decoder improves every prompt strategy, with the largest gain on edge prompts. That is the setting where subtle boundary interpretation matters most. The most straightforward reading is that useful visual information is already present in the frozen representation, but the default decoder does not use it well for camouflage.

Prompt robustness is the second key piece of evidence. Base SAM varies 3.5$\times$ across prompt types (0.205 to 0.727 mIoU), while the specialized decoder varies only 1.1$\times$ (0.694 to 0.776). \Cref{tab:prompt_ablation_viscon} shows that this is not an accidental by-product of fine-tuning: mixed prompt training consistently outperforms both point-only and box-only training. In particular, box-only training collapses on point prompts, which means robustness must be learned through exposure to multiple prompt modalities. The effect is not confined to GT-derived simulated clicks either: under a GT-free fixed center prompt, the specialized decoder still improves mIoU from 0.326 to 0.566 on the 2,026 camouflaged test images. This again points to a change in how the decoder uses subtle visual evidence, not just to better prompt handling.

\section{Discussion}
Our results do not show that the encoder never matters. The claim is narrower than that: for \emph{promptable camouflaged segmentation}, changing the decoder alone goes surprisingly far. The frozen encoder appears to already contain texture and edge information that is useful for camouflage reasoning, but the original decoder does not turn that information into masks reliably.

That is also why camouflage works well as a testbed for concept-oriented analysis. In ordinary scenes, strong semantic contrast can hide representational weaknesses. In camouflaged scenes, success depends on weak, spatially distributed evidence. When adapting only the decoder improves both accuracy and prompt robustness this much, the missing capability is likely downstream of feature extraction. That makes the decoder a natural place to study how subtle visual cues become segmentation decisions.
